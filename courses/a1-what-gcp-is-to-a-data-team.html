What GCP is to a data team

Google Cloud lists hundreds of products. A data team lives in a handful of them, and almost every decision a data leader signs off on is one of three questions: which project the work lives in, who may touch it, and which meter runs while somebody reads it. Tonight we draw the map: the organization, folders and projects that hold everything, where your bytes physically sit, the few services a data platform is built from, and the meters behind the bill.

Part 0

Where we start

Most Google Cloud introductions open the console and click through icons. A data leader needs a different first sentence: Google Cloud is a tree of containers, with an organization at the root and projects at the leaves, and the project is where three things meet: the bill, the permissions and the services. Once that sentence is clear, the six leader sessions are details of it. BigQuery as the warehouse and the lake, IAM down the tree, pipelines without servers, the bytes behind the bill, and one platform on a page. The eight builder sessions do the same work in the free BigQuery sandbox and on a laptop, on real Chicago taxi trips.

Part 1 · covers the resource hierarchy, projects and billing

A tree with the bill at the leaves

6 min live

Google describes the hierarchy in one line: "the organization (root) at the top, followed by folders (optional) for grouping, and then projects". Every resource except the root has exactly one parent. Datasets, buckets and jobs live inside projects, and a project is what Google calls essential for "enabling billing, adding and removing collaborators, and managing permissions". For a data team that makes the project the unit you budget, secure and hand to an owner.

LiveFolders are for people, projects are for money and access3 min▶

Folders are optional and exist to group projects the way your organisation thinks: by department, by environment, by team. Projects are not optional: "You need a project resource to use Google Cloud." A project carries the link to one billing account, the list of enabled services, and the permissions that apply to everything inside it. Google's own phrasing is that allow, deny and organization policies "are inherited through the hierarchy", so a grant on a folder reaches every project below it.

The practical rule for a data leader: one project per environment per workload is a sane default, because the project is the smallest box you can budget and lock down as a whole. Tidewater, the constructed grocery chain on every page that follows, has three: the lake in production, the BI workloads, and a development sandbox.

Self-studyBilling accounts sit beside the tree, not inside it2 min▶

A Cloud Billing account "defines who pays for a given set of Google Cloud resources", and one billing account can pay for many projects. The link is per project, which is why a cost report naturally breaks down by project and why mixing two teams' work in one project makes the bill hard to read. The BigQuery sandbox, used in the builder track, is the exception that proves the rule: a project with no billing account at all, and so with hard limits instead of a bill.

Real world

The single "data" project. A pattern repeated across many first cloud migrations: everything lands in one project because it is quicker. A year later the finance team cannot tell the BI dashboards' cost from the data science experiments, and the only way to give a contractor read access to one table is to hand-craft exceptions. Nothing was broken. The tree was never drawn. The story is a constructed composite of a common pattern.

Part 2 · covers regions, zones, multi-regions and the services

Where the bytes sit, and what reads them

5 min live

Google's wording: "Regions are independent geographic areas that consist of zones", and a region has "three or more zones housed in three or more physical data centers". BigQuery adds two multi-regions, US and EU, and Cloud Storage offers a region, a dual-region or a multi-region for every bucket. For Tidewater, with stores in Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia, the candidate regions are asia-southeast1 (Singapore), asia-southeast4 (Kuala Lumpur) and asia-southeast2 (Jakarta). The choice is made per dataset and per bucket, at creation.

Part 3 · covers the meters, the sandbox and the free trial

Storing is cheap, reading decides the bill

4 min live

A data leader does not need to memorise prices. They need to know which meter a decision turns on. BigQuery bills storage and compute separately: storage per GiB per month, compute either by the bytes each query reads (on-demand) or by slot-hours (capacity). Storage is the quiet meter. The reading meter scales with how people work, which is why the same table can be cheap for one team and expensive for the next.

LiveWhere the numbers come from2 min▶

BigQuery charges on-demand queries "for the number of bytes processed by each query", at $6.25 per TiB in the pricing page's default table (us-central1, read on 3 November 2026), with the first TiB each month free. The bytes come from BigQuery's documented data type sizes: 8 logical bytes for a number or timestamp, 2 plus the text's length for a string, nothing for a NULL. The public Chicago taxi table has 23 columns; measured on 18,362 real trips and scaled to its 211,670,894 rows, reading every column is 77.03 GiB, and reading three columns is 4.58 GiB. A dashboard that reruns the wide query 200 times a day bills 451.32 TiB a month, $2,814.50 after the free TiB. Builder session 8 computes every one of these numbers live, and session 5 explains the rest of the bill. Prices change: re-verify before relying on them.

Self-studyThe sandbox and the free trial, as they stand today2 min▶

Two ways in cost nothing. The BigQuery sandbox works "without providing a credit card or creating a billing account", gives the same 1 TiB of query processing a month as the free tier, and a "lifetime limit of 10 GiB of storage" that "is not refunded upon data deletion"; its tables expire after 60 days, and it does not support streaming, DML statements or the Data Transfer Service. The Free Trial is "$300 Welcome credit to spend over 90 days", and for it "you must provide a credit card or other payment method". Nothing in the leader track needs either. The builder track uses the sandbox plus a laptop, and marks every step that needs a billing account. Terms like these change: re-check both pages before you plan a workshop around them.

Real world

The estimate that priced the cheap meter. A team sizes its warehouse by storage: a few terabytes at cents per gigabyte a month, and the estimate looks small. The first month's bill is many times larger, because a BI tool runs SELECT * on the widest tables every few minutes and every refresh reads every column. The storage estimate was right. It measured the wrong meter. The scenario is constructed; the prices and byte counts are the real ones above.

Build-along

Your hierarchy, on one sheet

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No console and no account. A sheet of paper, the tree from Part 1, the engines from Part 2 and the meters from Part 3. You draw the organization, folders and projects your data work lives in today, or should live in, then mark for each project who pays, who owns it, and which meter it runs on. Session 6 turns this sheet into Tidewater's one-page platform, and yours.

Draw the root and the folders. One organization box. Under it, the folders your company would recognise: by environment, by department, or both. If you do not know whether your company has an organization resource, write "unknown, ask the platform team"; it changes what policies you can set later.

Put each data workload in a project. Where the raw and curated data live, where the dashboards run, where people experiment. One project per environment per workload is a good default. If two teams share one project today, draw it that way and circle it.

★ A finished row, in wordsFolder: data-platform Project: tw-lake-prod Holds: sales_raw, sales_curated, loyalty_pii datasets; the landing bucket Meter: bytes stored (storage) and bytes read (every query against it) Owner: data platform lead Pays: the Tidewater billing account

Tag each project with its main meter. Bytes stored, bytes read, slot-hours, or compute seconds. Circle any project where the meter scales with how often people click rather than how much data exists: those are next month's surprise.

Name an owner for every project. The person who answers when the bill jumps or the access is wrong. An empty owner cell is the most useful thing on the sheet.

Write the one sentence. In your own numbers or the ones from tonight: what your widest table costs to read once, and what it costs the way your dashboards read it. Session 5 checks the sentence against the full bill.

Ask "which project?" before "which service?". When someone proposes a new data workload on Google Cloud, the first question is where it lives in the tree, because that one answer decides who pays, who inherits access to it, and which policies already apply before anyone writes a line of code.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Find your organization and folders. Ask whoever administers Google Cloud whether an organization resource exists, and which folders sit under it. Add them to your sheet.
  2. Find the region. For your most important dataset, find its location. If the answer is a multi-region, note whether a residency rule applies to that data.
  3. Find the reading meter. Pick one dashboard on BigQuery and ask how often it refreshes and how many columns its query reads. You now have the number session 5 starts from.
  4. Bring the sheet. Session 2 fills in what lives inside the data project: datasets, tables, external tables and the bucket behind them.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Where do billing, permissions and enabled services all attach in Google Cloud?

Folders are optional groupings. The project links to one billing account, holds the enabled services, and carries permissions that apply to everything inside it, which makes it the natural unit to budget and own.

2 · Tidewater grants a role on the data-platform folder. Which resources does it reach?

Google's wording is that allow, deny and organization policies "are inherited through the hierarchy". A folder grant reaches tw-lake-prod and everything in it, and nothing in the sibling folders.

3 · SELECT * on the 77.03 GiB taxi table costs $0.4701 a run on demand; naming three columns costs $0.0280. What is the right reading?

On-demand charges follow "the total data processed in the columns you select". The table is the same; the habit changes the bytes, and session 5 adds partitions and clusters that cut them further.
