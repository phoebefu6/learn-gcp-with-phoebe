BigQuery is the warehouse and the lake

On most clouds the warehouse and the lake are two products, two bills and two sets of permissions. On Google Cloud one service plays both parts: BigQuery stores tables, and it also reads Parquet files sitting in a Cloud Storage bucket. Tonight we take that apart: the two meters behind every BigQuery bill, the three ways to reach the same files, the one-gate model BigLake gives you, and what changes for the team that no longer has a cluster to run.

Where we start

Session 1 put Tidewater's data in one project, tw-lake-prod. Tonight we open that project. Inside it sit BigQuery datasets that hold tables, and a bucket that holds files. The surprise for most leaders is how little separates the two: a BigQuery table can be data BigQuery stores itself, or a thin definition over files in the bucket. That one design choice decides how many copies of your data exist, how many permissions a reader needs, and which line of the bill grows when people get curious.

Part 1 · covers storage and compute billed apart, datasets and tables

One storage layer, two meters

6 min live

A BigQuery dataset is a container inside a project; tables live inside datasets. Underneath, BigQuery keeps the data in a columnar store and bills it on two meters that never touch each other. Storage is billed per GiB per month. Compute is billed either by the bytes each query processes (on-demand) or by slot-hours (capacity). The pricing page is explicit that "BigQuery uses a columnar data structure. You're charged according to the total data processed in the columns you select". So a query does not pay for the table. It pays for the columns it touches.

LiveStorage and compute are two bills3 min▶

BigQuery's pricing page describes two compute models. On demand, "you are charged for the number of bytes processed by each query". On capacity, compute is "measured in slots (virtual CPUs) over time". Storage sits on its own line: active or long-term, and logical or physical bytes, depending on the billing model you pick for the dataset. Nothing on the storage line changes when people run more queries, and nothing on the compute line changes when you keep more history.

For a leader this is the most useful fact about BigQuery. A decision to keep ten years of sales history is a storage decision, and it is cheap: active logical storage in us-central1 works out at "$23.552" for one TiB for a full month (BigQuery pricing page, 3 November 2026; prices change, re-verify before you quote them). A decision to put a wide dashboard in front of two hundred store managers is a compute decision, and it is the one that surprises people. On the public Chicago taxi table, the builder bench measures SELECT * at 77.03 GiB per run and three named columns at 4.58 GiB. Session 5 turns those bytes into the full bill.

Self-studyLong-term storage without moving anything2 min▶

BigQuery lowers the storage price on its own. When a table is not modified "for 90 consecutive days", its price "drops by approximately 50%", and the pricing page adds that there is "no difference in performance, durability, or availability" between active and long-term storage. Nobody exports anything, nobody moves anything, and queries read it exactly as before.

The consequence for planning: an archive strategy that copies cold BigQuery tables out to cheaper files to save money is often solving a problem BigQuery already solved, and adds a second copy to govern. The clock resets when the data is modified, so a job that rewrites a whole table every night keeps all of it on the active price. Ask how your tables are written before you ask what they cost to keep.

Real world

The archive project that saved half of a small line. A team spends a quarter building a job that exports year-old sales partitions out of the warehouse into cheap files, to cut the storage bill. Then someone reads the pricing page: untouched tables had already dropped to roughly half price on their own, and the storage line was a small share of the bill to begin with. The compute line, driven by a dashboard reading every column, was untouched by the project. The story is a constructed composite; the 90-day rule and the price ratio are Google's.

Part 2 · covers external tables, BigLake and Cloud Storage as the lake

Three ways to reach the same files

5 min live

Tidewater's point-of-sale files land in the bucket tw-lake-prod-landing as Parquet, one folder per day. BigQuery can reach them three ways. It can load them into a native table, which makes a second copy in BigQuery storage. It can define an external table: "External tables let you query structured data in external datastores." Or it can define a BigLake table, which does the same "with access delegation". The files are identical in all three cases. What changes is how many copies exist and how many doors a reader must pass.

LiveExternal or BigLake: one gate or two3 min▶

Google's introduction to external data sources puts the difference in one sentence: "Access delegation decouples access to the BigLake table from access to the underlying datastore." The BigLake page spells out the consequence: "you grant users access only to the BigLake table, not to the underlying datastore."

Read the other way round, that is the cost of a plain external table: without delegation, whoever queries it also needs to be able to read the files. That reading is ours, derived from Google's wording, but it matches what teams feel in practice. Every analyst who can query the external table can also open the bucket directly, outside any table definition, which is a second access path to govern. With BigLake, the bucket can stay closed to people and open only to the table.

Self-studyFormats and the folder layout BigQuery understands2 min▶

The BigLake page lists the formats it supports: Avro, CSV, Delta Lake, Iceberg, JSON, ORC and Parquet. The same page says BigLake tables reach "Cloud Storage, Amazon S3, and Blob Storage", so the lake does not have to be on Google Cloud at all.

When files are laid out in folders named after a key, BigQuery can read the key from the path. Google's own example of the default layout is gs://my_bucket/my_table/dt=2019-10-31/lang=en/my_filename; for Tidewater that becomes gs://tw-lake-prod-landing/sales/dt=2026-10-31/. The hive partitioned data page names three modes for the keys: AUTO, where "Key names and types are automatically detected", STRINGS, and CUSTOM. A query that filters on dt then opens only the matching folders. Builder session 1 builds exactly this layout on a laptop and counts the files a filter opens.

Choosing between Delta Lake, Iceberg and plain Parquet is a lakehouse decision, and it has its own home: Learn Databricks with Phoebe.

Real world

Two copies of the same table. The landing files are loaded into a native table every night for speed. A year later an upstream fix rewrites three months of files in the bucket, nobody reloads, and the BI team and the data science team, one reading the native table and one the files, present different revenue to the same steering committee. Both numbers were correct for the copy they read. The story is a constructed composite of a common pattern; the question it teaches is "which copy is the truth, and who keeps the other in sync?"

Part 3 · covers why a serverless warehouse changes team shape

No cluster to run, so the work moves

4 min live

There is no BigQuery cluster for your team to size, patch or resize. You create datasets, load or point at data, and pay the two meters. That is an argument about people as much as technology: the hours a platform team used to spend keeping machines healthy do not disappear, they move to the meters and the gates. The argument below is constructed reasoning, not a Google claim, and it carries no numbers.

LiveWhat the platform team stops doing, and starts doing2 min▶

Constructed reasoning, for discussion. When there is no cluster, the classic database-administration work of sizing, patching and rebalancing has no object. Three jobs grow in its place. First, cost governance: on-demand billing turns every analyst's query habit into a line on the bill, so somebody must own the reading meter, set guardrails and teach people to name columns. Second, access: with tables over files, the question "who can read the bucket, and who can only read the table?" becomes a design decision, not an afterthought. Third, modelling and contracts: when anyone can create a table in minutes, the hard part is agreeing which table is the truth.

The leadership move is to name an owner for each of the three before the migration, not after the first surprising invoice. Sessions 3 and 5 give the owners their tools: IAM down the hierarchy, and the full anatomy of the bill.

Self-studyWhere BigQuery is not the answer2 min▶

BigQuery is an analytical store: it is built to read many rows and few columns. It is not the place for an application's transactions, where each request writes or reads one row and must answer in milliseconds. Google Cloud has other products for those shapes; Bigtable and Spanner are the names you will hear, and these pages do not teach them. If a proposal puts a checkout system or a per-customer lookup API directly on BigQuery, ask what the read pattern is before anything else.

Two neighbouring topics have their own homes. How to model a warehouse (facts, dimensions, slowly changing history) is in Learn Data Warehouse with Phoebe. Open table formats and the lakehouse pattern are in Learn Databricks with Phoebe. What stays here is what is specific to BigQuery: the meters, the table types and the gates.

Real world

The administrator who became the cost owner. A retailer moves its warehouse to a serverless service and plans to let its database administrator go, since there is nothing left to administer. Three months later the same person is back in a new role: reading the query logs, finding the dashboard that reads every column every few minutes, and teaching analysts to name what they need. The skills transferred; the object changed. The story is a constructed composite.

Build-along

Native, external or BigLake: classify Tidewater's data

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Paper only. Take Tidewater's data, constructed for these pages: the landing files in tw-lake-prod-landing, and the datasets sales_raw, sales_curated and loyalty_pii in tw-lake-prod. For each one decide whether it should be a native table, an external table or a BigLake table, write the reason in one line, and list who still needs to read the bucket directly. Then do the same for three datasets of your own.

List what exists. Write one row per dataset or set of files: where it physically sits (BigQuery storage or a bucket path), and its format if it is files. For Tidewater: the landing files, sales_raw, sales_curated, loyalty_pii.

Work the first row together. sales_raw is the landing files as they arrived. Nobody edits them, many people read them, and nobody should need the bucket to do so.

★ Worked example: sales_rawData: sales_raw Sits in: gs://tw-lake-prod-landing/sales/dt=.../ (Parquet) Table type: BigLake table over the files Bucket: no person needs to read it directly Why: one copy, read in place; access delegation means "you grant users access only to the BigLake table"

Classify the other two. sales_curated is the cleaned, modelled table every dashboard reads; loyalty_pii holds member details. Decide the type for each and, more importantly, who must touch the bucket. A native table is a perfectly good answer when the data is produced inside BigQuery and read constantly; a copy is only a problem when it has a twin elsewhere.

★ One defensible answer for sales_curatedData: sales_curated Sits in: BigQuery storage, written by a scheduled SQL job Table type: native table Bucket: nobody; the data never lived in the bucket Why: produced inside BigQuery, read by every dashboard, no second copy anywhere to drift

Shorten the bucket column. Look down "who needs the bucket". Every person on that list is a second path to the data that bypasses the table. For each name, ask whether a BigLake table would remove them. The ideal list holds the pipeline's service account and nobody else.

Repeat for three of your own datasets. Use your company's names. Circle any data that exists twice, once as files and once loaded, and write who keeps the two in sync. If the answer is "nobody", that is the finding to take back to your team.

✗ The classification that doubles everythingload every file into a native table "for speed", keep the files too grant the whole analytics group Storage Object Viewer on the bucket "we will decide which copy is the truth later"

Count the gates before the copies. When someone proposes a new table over lake files, ask two questions in order: how many places will a reader be granted access, and how many copies of the data will exist? One and one is the target. Each extra answer is something a person must keep in sync by hand.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Tidewater turns an external table over its landing files into a BigLake table. What changes for an analyst like mei?

With access delegation, "you grant users access only to the BigLake table, not to the underlying datastore." The files stay where they are; the second gate goes away.

2 · On the Chicago taxi table, SELECT * processes 77.03 GiB and three named columns process 4.58 GiB. Why the difference on demand?

Both queries read all 211,670,894 rows. "You're charged according to the total data processed in the columns you select", so dropping 20 of 23 columns drops most of the bytes.

3 · When does a BigQuery table move to the long-term storage price?

Not modified "for 90 consecutive days", and the price "drops by approximately 50%", with "no difference in performance, durability, or availability". Nothing is moved and nothing is exported.
