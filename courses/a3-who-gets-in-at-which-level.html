Who gets in, at which level

Every access question on Google Cloud has two halves: which role, and where in the tree it is granted. A role granted on the organization reaches every project the company will ever create; the same role on one dataset reaches a handful of tables. Tonight we follow a permission down Tidewater's tree, size up the three kinds of role, meet the identities that are not people, and add the two tools that can say no: deny policies and Organization Policy.

Where we start

Session 2 ended on a list of who needs to read the bucket. That list is one small case of the question Identity and Access Management (IAM) answers for every request on Google Cloud: may this principal use this permission on this resource? Part 1 · covers allow policies inherited down the hierarchy

Grants flow down and add up

6 min live

An allow policy is a list of role bindings attached to one resource: an organization, a folder, a project, a dataset, a bucket. Each binding says "these principals hold this role here". Google's IAM overview puts the rule plainly: "descendant resources effectively inherit their ancestor resources' allow policies". So the access on any single table is everything granted on it, plus everything granted on every box above it.

LiveUnion, never subtract3 min▶

Google's policy inheritance page: "The effective allow policy for a resource is the union of the allow policy set at that resource and the allow policy inherited from its parent." The allow policies page adds the decision rule: "A specific access request to the resource is granted if any of the higher-level role bindings grant access to the request."

Put those two sentences together and an uncomfortable conclusion follows. Nothing you write in an allow policy lower down can take away what was granted higher up; a union only grows. Google does not state that conclusion in one sentence, so read it as our derivation from "union" and "any". The tool that does take access away is a different kind of policy, the deny policy in Part 3. For Tidewater it means the security group's Viewer at the organization reaches sales_curated.orders, and no dataset owner can turn it off from below.

Self-studyWhy a grant at the organization is the most expensive line you can write2 min▶

A binding's reach is everything below the resource it sits on. At a dataset that is a few tables. At a project it is every dataset, bucket and job inside. At the organization it is every folder and project that exists, and, by the same inheritance rule, every one created next year by teams you have not met yet. The cost of a grant is not the role alone; it is the role multiplied by the size of the subtree.

So the review question for any access request is "what is the lowest level where this grant still does the job?". Tidewater's analysts need to read curated sales, so they get BigQuery Data Viewer on the dataset sales_curated, not on the project and not on the folder. The few organization-level grants that remain, such as the security team's read-only view, are the ones to name in a policy document and re-review on a schedule.

Real world

The audit role that read the payroll. To give an external auditor visibility, a company grants a broad read role at the organization: quick, and it worked. Months later someone notices the same grant also reaches the HR project created after the audit began, including payroll exports. Nobody granted access to payroll. The tree did, by inheritance. The story is a constructed composite; the inheritance rule is Google's.

Part 2 · covers basic, predefined and custom roles, and service accounts

Three sizes of role, and identities that are not people

5 min live

A role is a bundle of permissions. Google sorts them into three kinds. Basic roles are "Highly permissive roles that provide broad access to Google Cloud services." Predefined roles are "managed by Google Cloud services", one service at a time. Custom roles "contain only the permissions that you specify". The difference in size is not subtle, and the figure draws it.

LiveBasic roles: useful in a test, dangerous in production3 min▶

Google's roles overview is unusually direct. Basic roles "include thousands of permissions across all Google Cloud services. In production environments, do not grant basic roles unless there is no alternative." There are two sets: the legacy Owner, Editor and Viewer, and newer basic roles named Admin, Writer and Reader. The legacy ones carry an extra limit: "you can't add conditions to role bindings for legacy basic roles", so you cannot even make them expire.

The predefined BigQuery roles show the alternative. Read from Google's role reference on 3 November 2026: BigQuery Data Viewer holds 27 permissions, BigQuery Data Editor 67, BigQuery Job User 11, BigQuery Admin 302. An analyst who reads curated sales needs Data Viewer on the dataset, plus Job User somewhere to run queries. The builder bench (builder session 8) models what one legacy Editor grant on the project does to that analyst instead: her permissions on one table go from 27 to 320 of the 334 permissions it models. Real Editor is larger still; the model only counts what it lists.

Self-studyService accounts, keys and impersonation2 min▶

A service account is "a special kind of account typically used by an application or compute workload, such as a Compute Engine instance, rather than a person", and it "is identified by its email address". Tidewater's ingestion job runs as ingest-pipeline@tw-lake-prod.iam.gserviceaccount.com, and it is granted roles exactly like a person or a group.

The risk lives in how a workload proves it is that account. A downloadable key works anywhere it is copied, which is why Google writes that "Service account keys are a security risk if not managed correctly" and that you "should choose a more secure alternative to service account keys whenever possible". One alternative is impersonation: a person or workload "authenticates as a service account to gain the service account's permissions", without a key file ever leaving Google Cloud. Part 3 shows the organization constraint that switches key creation off altogether.

Real world

The contractor who kept Editor. A contractor is given Editor on the production project for a two-week migration, because the predefined roles took too long to work out. The migration ends; the grant does not, since legacy basic roles cannot carry an expiry condition and nobody put a reminder in a calendar. A year later an access review finds the contractor's account still able to change almost anything in the project. The story is constructed; the no-conditions rule for legacy basic roles is Google's.

Part 3 · covers deny policies, IAM Conditions, and org policy as a different tool

The rules that say no, and the rules about what

4 min live

Allow policies only add. Three other tools narrow. A deny policy removes permissions from principals, whatever the allow policies say. An IAM Condition makes a single allow binding apply only when an expression is true. And Organization Policy is a separate service that limits what can be configured at all, whoever is asking. The order matters most, and Google states it in one line: "IAM always checks relevant deny policies before checking relevant allow policies."

LiveDeny policies: the floor a hurried grant cannot lower2 min▶

"IAM always checks relevant deny policies before checking relevant allow policies." A deny policy is "attached to an organization, folder, or project" and is inherited by everything below. Each rule names denied principals and exception principals, denied permissions and exception permissions, and an optional denial condition, which can only test resource tags. Permissions are written in the form SERVICE_FQDN/RESOURCE.ACTION; Google's own example is iam.googleapis.com/roles.delete. Each resource can carry up to 500 deny policies, holding 500 deny rules in total.

Tidewater's fence on loyalty data is one rule at the organization: everyone is denied bigquery.googleapis.com/tables.getData where the tag 1001/pii is true, except the privacy-officers group. lin, the loyalty lead, has BigQuery Data Viewer granted directly on loyalty_pii. She is still denied, because step 1 ends the evaluation before her grant is read. That rule is ours, constructed for Tidewater; the order is Google's.

Self-studyIAM Conditions: a grant that applies only sometimes2 min▶

A condition is an expression attached to one allow binding, written in "Common Expression Language, or CEL". Google's examples: request.time < timestamp('2021-01-01T00:00:00Z'), a grant that expires, and resource.name.startsWith('projects/_/buckets/exampleco-site-assets/'), a grant limited to part of a resource. Tidewater uses both: the contractors' grant on the dev project ends on 31 December 2026, and the ingestion service account may edit data only in sales_raw.

Two limits. "Only some resource types accept conditions in role bindings", so check before you design around one. And "You cannot use conditions when you grant legacy basic roles, including Owner (roles/owner), Editor (roles/editor), and Viewer (roles/viewer)", which is one more reason the contractor in Part 2 kept Editor.

Self-studyOrganization Policy: rules about what, not who2 min▶

Google separates the two tools in a pair of sentences. IAM "focuses on who, and lets the administrator authorize who can take action on specific resources". Organization Policy "focuses on what, and lets the administrator set restrictions on specific resources to determine how they can be configured." A constraint applies whoever is asking, administrators included.

Three constraints a data leader should know by name. gcp.resourceLocations "defines the set of locations where location-based Google Cloud resources can be created", the residency guardrail. iam.disableServiceAccountKeyCreation "disables the creation of service account external keys". storage.uniformBucketLevelAccess "requires buckets to use uniform bucket-level access", which builder session 1 explains. If Tidewater's data must stay in South-East Asia, the reliable answer is a location constraint, not a reminder to everyone with Editor.

Real world

The bucket that could not be created. An engineer on a deadline tries to create a bucket in a US multi-region to share files with a vendor, and the console refuses. Nobody had to review the request: a location constraint set once at the organization already said where data may live. The story is constructed; the constraint and its wording are Google's.

Build-along

Tidewater's access plan, as bindings

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Paper only. Write Tidewater's access plan the way IAM stores it: one row per binding with who, which role, where in the tree, and any condition; then one deny rule that fences the loyalty data. Tidewater, its people and its policies are constructed for these pages. When you have finished, the same plan is evaluated case by case in builder session 4 and on the bench in builder session 8, which checks 27 cases against Google's documented order.

List the people and workloads. For Tidewater: mei (analysts), raj (data-eng), ana (contractors), zoe (privacy-officers), sam (security), lin (loyalty lead), and the ingestion service account. Grant to groups, not to individuals, wherever a group exists.

Write one binding per job, at the lowest level that still works. Start with the analysts and the ingestion pipeline.

★ Worked rowsWHO ROLE WHERE CONDITION group analysts BigQuery Data Viewer dataset sales_curated none group analysts BigQuery Job User folder analytics none ingest-pipeline (SA) BigQuery Data Editor project tw-lake-prod name starts with .../datasets/sales_raw group contractors BigQuery Data Editor project tw-lake-dev request.time < 2026-12-31

Write the deny rule for the loyalty data. Fill all five fields. Ask who must be an exception, and remember an exception only skips the deny; it grants nothing on its own.

★ Worked deny ruleAttached to: organization tidewater.example Denied principals: everyone Exception principals: group privacy-officers Denied permissions: bigquery.googleapis.com/tables.getData bigquery.googleapis.com/tables.export Denial condition: resource tag 1001/pii = true

Trace two requests by hand. First mei reading sales_curated.orders: no deny matches, the analysts' dataset binding grants it, Allowed. Then lin reading loyalty_pii.members: the deny matches and she is not an exception, Denied, and her direct grant is never read. If your trace disagrees, find the step you skipped.

Start the sheet for your own platform. Copy the four columns. Mark every row granted at a folder or the organization, and every basic role. Those are the rows to defend first in your next access review.

✗ The access plan that cannot say nogrant Editor on the project to the data team "so nobody is blocked" grant Viewer at the organization to anyone who asks for a report protect the PII dataset by "not telling people it exists"

Ask "at which level?" in every access review. The role name gets all the attention, but the level decides the reach. A Data Viewer at the organization is a bigger grant than an Admin on one dataset. Read every plan by where the bindings sit before you read what they say.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Count the basic roles. Ask your platform team how many bindings in production use Owner, Editor or Viewer. Each one is a candidate for a predefined role.
  2. Find the highest grant. Name the broadest binding at your organization or top folder. Write down who holds it and when it was last reviewed.
  3. Find one service account key. Ask whether any service account has a downloadable key, and whether key creation is switched off by organization policy.
  4. Draft one deny rule. For your most sensitive dataset, write the five fields. Bring it to session 4, where the pipelines that read and write that data run as service accounts.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Tidewater grants the data-eng group BigQuery Data Editor on the folder data-platform. What does it reach?

IAM: "descendant resources effectively inherit their ancestor resources' allow policies." The grant reaches everything below data-platform, and nothing in the sibling folders analytics and sandbox.

2 · lin holds BigQuery Data Viewer directly on loyalty_pii. An organization deny rule denies everyone except privacy officers tables.getData on tables tagged pii. lin is not a privacy officer. What does IAM answer?

"IAM always checks relevant deny policies before checking relevant allow policies." There is no specificity rule: the inherited deny matches, lin is not an exception, and step 1 ends the evaluation.

3 · Tidewater wants no bucket or dataset ever created outside Asia, whoever asks. Which tool fits?

IAM "focuses on who"; Organization Policy "focuses on what". A location constraint applies to every principal, including administrators with broad roles.
