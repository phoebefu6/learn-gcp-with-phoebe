Part 1 · covers the evaluation order the evaluator implements

Deny first, then the union of everything above

6 min live

Google states the order in one sentence: "IAM always checks relevant deny policies before checking relevant allow policies." Deny policies attach to an organization, folder or project and "are inherited by all lower-level resources". Allow policies are then read from the resource up: "The effective allow policy for a resource is the union of the allow policy set at that resource and the allow policy inherited from its parent." If nothing grants the permission, it is not granted. The evaluator on this page follows those three steps for one stated scope.

LiveWhat the evaluator does, and refuses to do3 min▶

A deny rule applies when the principal is a denied principal and not an exception principal, the permission is denied and not an exception permission, and the denial condition is true or absent. Deny permissions use Google's format SERVICE_FQDN/RESOURCE.ACTION, such as bigquery.googleapis.com/tables.updateData, and permission groups such as storage.googleapis.com/objects.*. Denial conditions "only recognize resource tag functions", so the evaluator reads resource.matchTag against tags inherited down the tree. Allow bindings grant when the principal or one of its groups is a member, the role holds the permission, and the IAM Condition is true; conditions are a small CEL subset: resource.name.startsWith, request.time compared with a timestamp, !, &&, ||.

It does not model principal access boundary policies, custom roles, organization policy constraints, BigQuery's dataset access lists, or any CEL beyond that subset, and it throws rather than guessing on anything it does not know. The predefined roles carry Google's own permission lists, read from the roles reference on 3 November 2026: Data Viewer 27 permissions, Data Editor 67, Data Owner 101, Job User 11, BigQuery User 41, BigQuery Admin 302, Storage Object Viewer 8, Storage Object Admin 32. The legacy basic roles are modelled, because Google lists their thousands of permissions only in the console: Viewer as the read-only verbs, Editor as everything except setting IAM policy, Owner as all. The same logic runs in Python, and on all 27 cases in three modes plus the anti-lever counts, Python and the browser agree.

Self-studyWhere the 27 expected verdicts come from2 min▶

Tidewater's hierarchy, people and policies are constructed, so no case is copied from Google. Each case instead applies one documented rule, quoted beside it in the table: the union of inherited allow policies, deny before allow, deny attachment points and inheritance, exception principals and exception permissions, a condition that turns false after a date, a resource.name.startsWith condition that fails for a sibling dataset, and "You cannot use conditions when you grant legacy basic roles", which the two binding cases test. Several cases are traps worth reading: c16, where a contractor may delete in the dev project because the deny sits on a different folder, and c27, where an exception principal is still not granted anything, because "exempt from the deny rule" is not the same as allowed.

Part 2 · covers the anti-lever and the break buttons

One basic role, and two ways to be wrong

5 min live

The anti-lever is the most common shortcut in real projects: someone is blocked, a deadline is close, and Editor goes on the project. Google's own advice is the opposite: basic roles "include thousands of permissions across all Google Cloud services. In production environments, do not grant basic roles unless there is no alternative." The break buttons are the opposite test: they make the evaluator wrong on purpose, so you can see that the 27 cases measure the logic and do not merely agree with it.

LiveThe anti-lever, with its honest nuance2 min▶

With Editor on tw-lake-prod, the request that was not granted, mei reading sales_raw.orders, is allowed. So is almost everything else in the project: on one table her permissions go from 27 to 320 of the 334 the bench models, including deleting tables and models, updating routines and writing objects in the landing bucket. Across the 25 requests, allowed verdicts rise from 10 to 13.

The nuance matters: Editor does not beat a deny. Case c26, mei writing to a prod table, stays denied, because the project's deny rule on writes is checked before any allow is read. That is the real lesson of deny policies: they are the floor a hurried grant cannot lower. It is also why least privilege is a practice and not a slogan. The concepts behind it, roles, attributes and access reviews, are taught in data access control. And the counts are modelled for Editor: the real role holds thousands of permissions across all services, so 320 is a floor, not a ceiling.

LiveWhat the break buttons prove2 min▶

Evaluate allow before deny fails exactly the 3 cases where a deny meets an allow: raj's write to prod (c06), lin's direct grant on the pii table (c12) and sam's read of it under an organization-wide Viewer (c21). Ignore inheritance fails 14, every case decided by a grant or a deny above the resource itself, from the analysts' dataset grant (c01) to the contractor fence on the folder (c17). If either button had left the score at 27, the cases would be decoration. They are not.

Build-along · the cost half

Meter one question four ways

★ 14 min · the main event, part two

The question is "average fare by pickup area in January 2019" on the public Chicago taxi table. The ladder asks it as SELECT *, then with three named columns, then against a copy partitioned by day, then against a copy that is also clustered by pickup area with a filter on Midway. Click a rung to select it, then set how often it runs and compare a month of on-demand with a slot reservation. Build your own query underneath from any of the 23 columns.

Read it in this order. Processed bytes first, because they do not depend on any price, then billed, then dollars. Tick LIMIT 10 on any rung and watch nothing change. Select the clustered rung and notice that billed is larger than processed: that is the 10 MB minimum.

Part 3 · covers how the meter computes bytes, and what is modelled

Logical bytes, columns, partitions, blocks

5 min live

BigQuery charges on-demand queries by "the total data processed in the columns you select", and it sizes that data by type: 8 logical bytes for INT64, FLOAT64 and TIMESTAMP, 2 plus the UTF-8 length for a STRING, and "A NULL value for any data type is calculated as 0 logical bytes". The meter applies exactly those rules to 18,362 real trips, averages per column, and scales by 211,670,894 / 18,362 = 11,527.7 to the full table. Then "Charges are rounded up to the nearest MB, with a minimum 10 MB data processed per table referenced by the query, and with a minimum 10 MB data processed per query."

LiveWhy clustering is modelled, and what the dry run tells you2 min▶

Partitions are exact: the January 2019 rung reads the portal's exact count of trips in those 31 days, 1,213,529. Clustering is not something Google lets you count in advance. "Clustered tables in BigQuery sort data in storage blocks based on the values in user-defined clustered columns", and "Because the number of blocks to scan isn't known until a query runs, you don't receive an accurate cost estimate before execution." The meter therefore models it: each day's trips, sorted by pickup area with NULLs first, cut into blocks of 5,000 rows, and a filter on area 56 reads every block that holds any Midway trip, 36 of 257. The real saving will differ. On a real clustered table, the dry run's estimate "is an upper bound, and can be higher than the actual number of bytes billed", which is also why a maximum_bytes_billed ceiling can stop a clustered query that would have fit.

Self-studyOn-demand or slots: what the monthly panel assumes2 min▶

At 200 runs a day, the SELECT * rung bills 451.32 TiB a month, $2,814.50 on demand after the free TiB, and the named-column rung 26.85 TiB, $161.55. The partitioned rung stays inside the free TiB. A 100-slot Standard reservation billed 8 hours a day for 30 days is $960.00 at $0.04 per slot-hour. That last number rests on an assumption the panel labels: real autoscaling "always autoscale[s] to a multiple of 50" and bills slot-seconds used, with a one-minute minimum, and whether 100 slots finish your workload in time is a performance question the meter does not answer. Standard edition also has "No access to BigQuery ML", which matters for session 7. Every price is the pricing page's default table, us-central1, read on 3 November 2026: re-verify before delivery.

Real world

The dashboard that read the hash column. A BI tool generated SELECT * behind every tile, so every refresh carried the wide identifier columns no chart ever showed. Replacing the tile queries with named columns and a date filter on a partitioned copy turned the dominant line of the bill into a rounding error. The story is a constructed composite; the byte counts are the meter's.

Homework

After the bench

30 min
  1. Find a case the evaluator gets "wrong" for your company. Pick one real grant at work, write it as a request, and list what the bench does not model that would change the verdict: a custom role, a principal access boundary, an organization policy.
  2. Price your own widest query. In "Your own query", tick the columns your busiest dashboard reads. Write down processed bytes with and without the partition filter.
  3. Find the break-even. Select the named-column rung and raise runs a day until on-demand passes the $960.00 reservation. Then write one sentence on why that number alone should not decide the purchase.
  4. Re-run the two numbers in the sandbox. Dry-run the session 2 query on the real table and compare its estimate with the meter's named-column rung. Note the difference, and which one you would trust for a budget.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and builder session 8 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · lin has BigQuery Data Viewer granted directly on loyalty_pii, and an organization deny rule covers reads of pii-tagged data for everyone except privacy officers. What does IAM answer?

Specificity does not exist in this model. The deny rule is inherited from the organization, lin is not an exception principal, the tag matches, so step 1 ends the evaluation (case c12).

2 · What do the two numbers beside the anti-lever say?

The two-number headline is the point: the fix and its cost. And because deny is checked first, case c26 stays denied with Editor in place.

3 · The clustered rung processes 3.8 MB but bills 10 MB. Why?

Below 10 MB every query on one table bills the same. The cluster saving is real in bytes read but, at this size, invisible on the bill; and it is modelled, because the real block layout is not public.
